We [asked](/blog/what-if-every-shard-were-a-raft-group.html) what it would take for every Felix shard to be a Raft group, and wrote down what Raft had to prove before it replaced anything. This post is what happened next. Raft is the default shard engine: with no setting, every durable shard runs as its own [openraft](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft) group, a standalone broker included, as a group of one. The protocol we built ourselves, which our docs call the hybrid, is frozen and on its way out of the tree.

The interesting part is not the switch. It is what the old protocol kept getting wrong, and what we still own now that Raft does the agreeing.

## The bugs had one shape

Several posts here are about single defects in the hybrid: [a follower that lost its disk](/blog/the-follower-that-forgot.html), [a trim that hid a fence](/blog/the-trim-that-hid-the-fence.html), [a fence asked to answer two questions](/blog/two-questions-one-fence.html), [waits that never ended](/blog/silence-is-not-evidence.html), and [records acknowledged and then lost](/blog/acknowledged-then-forgotten.html) under periodic fsync. Read side by side, they share a shape. Each rule in the protocol was sound on its own. The bugs lived where two rules met.

The biggest meeting point was the question of who leads. In the hybrid, the control plane named a shard's leader and gave it a generation. When the control plane was down, brokers could elect a leader among themselves, so failover did not depend on it. That is two authorities for one fact, and most of the hard bugs came from the moments when they disagreed or waited on each other.

The rest of the list reads the same way. A restarted leader's shards stayed fencing because they had no earlier replica set to fence against, and a guard added for a different bug refused every rename that could have freed them ([#1966](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/1966)). A leaderless shard could wait forever on an old replica set whose members had been decommissioned ([#1890](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/1890)). With brokers electing their own leaders, shards moved off a killed broker never healed, even after it came back ([#1914](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/1914)). Failover itself still waited on the control plane's lease ([#1009](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/1009)). Even the model had the problem: two actions in the TLA+ spec dropped the leader's own kept set from the joint fence ([#1990](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/1990)).

None of these is a wrong idea. Each is two right ideas, added months apart, that nobody had run against each other. Every fix was another rule, and the next bug was where that rule met an older one. That is the cost we [worried about](/blog/what-if-every-shard-were-a-raft-group.html) before we had the numbers, and it is the reason we stopped.

## What Raft took off our hands

Under Raft, for a durable shard, the control plane places the replica set and nothing more. Who leads is decided inside the group, by an election whose rules have been studied by many more people than us. Most of the hybrid's machinery has a plain counterpart:

- The control plane's generation becomes the Raft term, which each record already carries in its header.
- A promoted leader no longer runs a fence before it serves. It opens when the blank entry of its term commits.
- A copy that lost its disk is no longer vouched back in. It gets a new copy id, joins as a new member, and starts as a learner.
- A read that must not be stale no longer asks the control plane who leads. It runs a read-index round.
- A dead leader is noticed by Raft's heartbeats and election timeout, not reported through the control plane.
- A cache shard no longer keeps a cache log and a separate counter log. It has one log and one group.

The [retirement plan](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/blob/main/docs/hybrid-retirement.md) counts what goes with it: about 21,000 lines of non-test replication code, 2,000 lines of storage code, 8 TLA+ specs with 426 model configurations, and about 7,800 lines of cluster tests written for the old protocol. Eleven environment variables go. So do the six fleet features an operator had to finalize, one at a time, to roll the protocol's rules forward. Of 56 internal message kinds, 34 exist only for the hybrid.

The numbers are not the point, though. The point is that the question "who leads this shard?" has one answer, and the thing that answers it is not ours.

## What it cost

We wrote the gate down first: on every three-replica cell, Raft had to be at least as fast as what it replaced, within the noise, and use no more than 10% more broker CPU per message ([#2646](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2646)). The first measurements did not pass. With periodic fsync, Raft was 14 to 19% behind on `Quorum` streams and 30% behind on `Leader` streams.

None of the gaps were in consensus. They were in how Felix fed it.

The largest was fsync. openraft counts an entry toward a commit only once the log says it is flushed, and our first Raft log fsynced to say so on every commit, whatever fsync mode the broker was set to. A broker on periodic fsync, which normally lets one flush cover many writes, was suddenly flushing per commit.

The fix was to let a Raft log on a relaxed broker count an entry once it is written, and leave the fsync to the periodic flusher, so one flush serves every commit since the last. Votes are still synced before they are answered. That is safe only because a broker in that mode rotates its copy ids after a crash, so a copy that might have lost what it counted comes back as a new member and never votes on its old promises.

Look at what that fix is, though. It is a Felix rule about when a write counts, sitting next to Raft's. [Acknowledged, then forgotten](/blog/acknowledged-then-forgotten.html) is about exactly this kind of rule going wrong in the hybrid. So it has its own TLA+ model, and it stays one of the places we look first when something is off.

The other gaps were smaller and just as unglamorous. Raft spent 1.12 to 1.31 times the hybrid's CPU per message on the three-replica throughput cells. One pull request gathered the fixes: fewer task hops and wake-ups per write, a log thread that parks when quiet, groups on their own threads, a follower's written report carried on its append answer instead of a separate message ([#2960](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/pull/2960)). After that work, against the release it replaced, Raft used 14% fewer instructions per message at 1,000 messages a second, 28% fewer flat out, and the same within the noise at 100 a second ([#2936](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2936)). In one matrix run, Kafka produce held 54k messages a second on Raft against 207k on the hybrid, because each record of an idempotent producer became its own proposal and waited for the one before it ([#2704](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2704)).

Then there is having thousands of groups on one broker. A vote is fsynced, and every group has its own vote file. On a development machine, 40 groups formed at the same moment had not finished electing after 180 seconds: each round of vote saves outlasted the election timeout, so the timeouts fired again and added more saves, and one member reached term 23 ([#2317](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2317)). A broker bounds how many of its groups are in an election at once, 64 by default, and lowers the bound when elections are losing. Groups that took writes go first. Idle groups still heartbeat, less often than busy ones. Letting idle groups sleep is a design of its own, with its own failure modes, and we left it out.

## What stays ours

Raft answers two questions well: who leads, and which entries are committed. It is silent on several others, and every one of them turned into Felix code.

**Copy identity.** Raft assumes a member that voted remembers its vote. A broker whose shard directory was wiped does not, and would happily vote again under the same id from an empty log. Each shard directory mints its own copy id, every Raft request names the id it is for, and a copy refuses a request addressed to any other id, including an older one of its own.

**The restore check.** A directory restored from a backup, or a VM rolled back, keeps its copy id with an older log and an older vote. Raft cannot see that at all. A gate in front of Raft compares each data root with evidence kept elsewhere, and a root that is behind gets new ids before any of its members vote.

**Re-forming.** Raft has no answer when a group loses its majority, say two of three disks at once. The group stays down until an operator re-forms it, and re-forming has rules of its own. One of them came from a real bug: a shard whose every copy had rotated was formed again at index 0 and would have handed out offsets it had already acknowledged ([#2620](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2620)).

**The lease, the written commit and placement.** `Leader` streams acknowledge on the leader's log while its lease holds. Relaxed brokers count a written majority. The control plane still decides which brokers hold a copy, and membership changes have to follow it.

Most of the Raft-era fixes we have merged are in these edges: placement that tops up a short member set, a re-formed group's new member catching up, a cache watch that resumes on another member after a failover, a read right after an acknowledged publish that must include it. Very few are about who leads.

The models moved the same way. The Raft specs are not about Raft's election. They are about identity, the lease, re-forming, restores, placement and the written commit. By line count they are larger than the hybrid's were. We think that is the right place for the effort: the models check the parts nobody else checks for us.

Everything users relied on had to be rebuilt on the new engine too: conditional publish, head trim, atomic commits, Kafka produce with idempotent sequences, MQTT, caches and counters, cache watches. Most behave exactly as before. A few show the seams. A refused conditional publish consumes one offset on Raft, a gap readers skip, because the leader decides the condition at the index the entry lands at. In-memory streams have no log, so they never become a group and got a serving path of their own ([#3065](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/3065)). And a cluster made on the old engine does not convert. It starts again on empty directories.

## What we learned

**A protocol you build grows by exception.** Every rule we added had a failing test behind it. The trouble was never one rule. It was the number of pairs of rules, and that grows faster than the rules do.

**Two authorities for one fact is where the bugs go.** Many of the hybrid's hard bugs were the control plane and the brokers disagreeing about who leads, or waiting on each other to find out. Raft did not fix that by being clever. It fixed it by being the only one asked.

**Borrowing consensus does not mean borrowing correctness for the whole system.** Raft is correct about Raft. Storage identity, restores, re-forming and the rules you add for speed are still yours, and they are where the next bugs will be. Put the models and the tests there.

**The cost of the swap is in the glue.** Every performance gap we traced was in how Felix called the library: when it fsynced, how many tasks it woke, how it split a request into proposals. None needed a change to Raft.

**Write the bar down before the numbers.** The gate existed before the first measurement, so a gap was a bug to fix, not a judgment call. Without it, a 14% drop would have been easy to explain away. With it, the drop was a list of work.

Building the first version ourselves was not wasted. It taught us what Felix's replication had to guarantee, which is the knowledge we needed to see that Raft guaranteed most of it already. Once that was clear, the right move was to stop owning the part that everyone else had already gotten right.
