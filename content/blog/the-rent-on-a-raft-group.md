In Felix every shard is its own Raft group, built on [openraft](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft). [What if every shard were a Raft group?](/blog/what-if-every-shard-were-a-raft-group.html) explains why. This post is about what happens next, when one broker holds a member of every shard it replicates and that turns out to be thousands of groups in one process.

A single Raft group is cheap. It wakes a few times a second, writes a vote now and then, and keeps a few queues. None of that is worth measuring until you multiply it. At a few thousand groups, every small per-group habit becomes a line on the broker's CPU, memory or disk budget, and some of them interact in ways no single group would ever show.

Call it rent: small, due every interval, and charged per group. Here is where it hid.

## A clock per group

openraft gives each group its own ticker, which wakes the group on an interval to check its heartbeat and election timers. Measured on openraft 0.10.0, with one process leading every group on a 4-vCPU runner, that came to 86% of a core per 1,000 members with the default timers, while the groups did nothing at all.

The fix was to take the clocks away. openraft 0.10.1 added `Trigger::tick()`, which runs one timer check from outside ([openraft#2305](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft/pull/2305)), and moved elections onto a deadline of their own ([openraft#2343](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft/pull/2343)). Felix builds every group with both internal timers off, and runs one task per broker, the node ticker, that drives them all. With the internal tick off, idle CPU from ticking went to zero.

One clock is only half of it. The ticker keeps, for each group, the time it is next due, and only touches those. A leader is due when its next heartbeat is. A follower is due when its election timeout could have run out, counted from the last message it accepted from its leader, so a follower that keeps hearing its leader is never ticked at all.

A group that takes no writes for 5 s also slows down. Its leader heartbeats every 2 s instead of every 250 ms, and its followers wait longer before they stand. The trade is plain: a dead leader of an idle group is noticed in 6 to 7 s rather than 3 to 4. A group that takes writes keeps the short timers. The rules that keep a slow leader from being deposed by its own impatient followers are longer than this post, and they are in [per-shard Raft, "Idle groups"](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/blob/main/docs/per-shard-raft.md#idle-groups).

Ticking only due groups did not get idle CPU to nothing, because an idle group still heartbeats. On the group runtime (Linux, release build, three runtimes in one process as three brokers, RF 3, a 1 s idle heartbeat), 1,500 members cost 2.1% to 2.3% of a core per broker and 2,000 cost 2.8%. A profile put about nine tenths of that inside openraft: a heartbeat round wakes the leader's core task three times and each follower's once, and every wake rebuilds and republishes that member's metrics. That is a sensible design for a process with one group, where fresh metrics are worth a few microseconds. With thousands of groups it is most of the bill. A 2 s idle heartbeat brought it to 1.2%.

The lesson we would pass on: count wakes, not work. An idle group does almost nothing per wake, so the number of wakes is the whole cost.

## One envelope per peer

At RF 3 with 2,000 members, a broker leads about 667 groups, so it sends about 1,300 heartbeats to its peers every interval and answers about as many. Each is an empty append, and each was its own message.

Felix wraps the transport so that a heartbeat waits up to 1 ms for others going to the same broker, then all of them travel as one message, answered with one result per heartbeat in order. openraft leaves this to the application and shows the pattern in its multi-group example ([openraft#2390](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft/pull/2390)). Because the one ticker checks every due group on the same tick, their heartbeats land in the same window without any extra effort. A batch holds at most 512 heartbeats, and an append that carries entries never waits. In the unit harness, 24 groups at the short heartbeat cost each broker 72 messages a second on their own and 6 together.

The window can only make a lease shorter, never longer. A leader's lease counts from when openraft handed off the heartbeat, which is before the wait. A follower's promise not to vote for anyone else counts from when it takes the heartbeat, which is after it. So the window costs a little liveness and no safety.

Batching does have a cost we did not see coming, which is that it ties groups to each other.

## The lease read that waited on its neighbours

One of our scale tests creates 600 streams in bulk while a lease cache, itself a Raft group, renews leases and reads them back. Every read has to finish within 2 s. On Raft it kept failing ([#3032](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/3032)), with reads in one diagnostic run reaching 3.6 s.

The first cause was ordinary. Opening a shard's Raft copy locked its directory, synced a few files and opened the log, all on a runtime worker thread, and a broker opened about 600 of them in a row. A worker sat blocked in fsyncs for the whole load window, and the peer transport and heartbeat tasks queued behind it. [#3353](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/pull/3353) moved that file work to blocking threads, in the same order. Half the shards were serving at 39 s instead of 73 to 98 s, but some reads were still slow.

The second cause was the batch. While shards were opening, followers answered heartbeat batches about 370 ms late, a stall shared by the whole broker. The receiving side of a batch gave every group 1 ms after the first answer and then answered the rest as slow, which the leader counts as not answered. The lease group's heartbeat was refused that way in 413 of 925 batches. Each refusal counted as a failed answer, so read-index rounds that needed the lease group's heartbeat started over, taking 0.5 to 1.8 s.

[#3377](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/pull/3377) made the wait a quarter of the heartbeat, 50 ms by default, counted from the first answer and cut short in time to get the reply back before the batch's deadline. On the scale test the worst read under load went from 3.6 s to 727 ms, with a p99 of 718 ms, and the slowest lease renewal from 2.2 s to 571 ms.

The price is written down too. One group that is always slow now adds up to 50 ms to the read-index rounds of the healthy groups that share its batches. Writes are not affected, and every answer in a batch is still that group's own. We took that trade because the alternative was failing whole rounds every time the broker hiccupped.

The lesson: once you batch, a group's latency depends on its neighbours. Decide on purpose how long one straggler may hold the rest, and pick a number that matches how a real stall looks, not how a single slow group looks.

## Elections that feed themselves

A Raft vote has to be on disk before it is answered. In Felix that means writing a small ballot file and renaming it into place, which is an fsync of the file and one of its directory. For one group this is nothing. For a broker whose groups all lose their leader together, after a crash or a restart or a bulk create, it is a few thousand of them in the same second.

That turns into a loop. If the disk cannot get through a round of vote saves inside the election timeout, the timeouts fire again, each adding more saves to a queue that was already too long, and no group settles ([#2317](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2317)). On a development Mac, where an fsync is `F_FULLFSYNC`, 40 groups told to form at the same moment had not finished electing after 180 s, with about 120 threads blocked in fsync and one member at term 23. Formed one after another, the same 40 elected in a few seconds. On a 4-vCPU CI runner, 2,000 groups formed at once had no leader at all after 180 s. On a 32-vCPU Linux machine with local NVMe, 40 groups at once were all led in about 2 s, which is why a small test on a fast machine says nothing about this.

Two changes went in. A vote at a new term had been writing both the ballot and the shard's replica state, four fsyncs in all. The ballot alone makes the vote durable, so [#3015](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/pull/3015) dropped the second write and halved the fsyncs of nearly every vote in a storm.

The bigger change bounds how many members on a broker are in an election at once ([#2831](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/pull/2831)). `FELIX_SHARD_RAFT_ELECTIONS_AT_ONCE` sets it, 64 by default. A member whose wait for its leader has run out joins a queue, and the ticker turns its election timer on only when a place is free. Groups that were taking writes go ahead of idle ones. Within each class the order is a hash of group and member, so two brokers go through their groups in different orders and one member of a group usually wins before the others get a place.

The bound moves with the disk. While elections are losing and vote saves run more than four times slower than one that had the disk to itself, the limit drops to what the disk can save in that time, never below 8. Once elections win again it climbs back, up to four times the setting. A second rule watches the queue of saves itself, since a round that gave up leaves its saves behind, and lets no new member in while that queue would take more than a quarter of the shortest election timeout to drain. A group that loses its leader on its own is never held back, because a place is free.

What keeps this safe is that the bound only chooses when to turn a member's election timer on. openraft still picks the moment it stands and runs the vote as it always does. Holding a member back looks, to the protocol, exactly like a timeout that happened to be longer.

A deterministic model runs the real bound against two live brokers whose groups all lost their leader to a third, on a mock clock, with one disk per broker that saves one vote at a time at 5 ms an fsync:

With the bound, a group takes about one election, and the time until every group is led is just the time the disks need to save its votes. Without it, members that cannot get their vote saved in time stand again, and about four times the elections take more than twice as long. It is a model, and it leaves out CPU and the slower macOS sync, so it tells you the shape rather than how a real broker will do.

The lesson: when thousands of groups share one disk, an election timeout is a deadline on a shared queue. Bound how many groups enter it at once, and let the bound follow what the disk is actually doing.

## Where the rest of the cost hides

The timers, heartbeats and votes were the loud ones. The rest showed up only when we multiplied something small by a few thousand.

**Queues that walk.** openraft preallocated its engine's command queue for 4,096 commands and used it first in, first out. A `VecDeque` does not move its head back to the start when it empties, so an idle group that runs about one command a second slowly touched every page of a 720 KB buffer. One group never notices. A process with 1,500 idle groups grew about 15 MB of resident memory a minute ([openraft#2312](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft/issues/2312)). The preallocation was there for good reasons, so the fix that went upstream keeps it and resets a queue's head when it drains ([openraft#2313](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft/pull/2313)), and a second change starts the queues small and grows them ([openraft#2314](https://github.com/databendlabs/openraft/pull/2314)). Felix's memory test went from 414 KB of live heap per idle member to about 150 KB, and a 1,500-member broker in the three-runtime run held 232 MB resident, 17 MB of it the logs.

**Caches sized for one.** Each log keeps its newest records in memory, up to 4 MiB, so replication reads fresh entries without touching segments. That is a good default for a log. At 2,000 groups that had each taken writes, it is 8 GB. The shard logs on a broker now share one budget, 256 MiB by default, and idle groups give up their bytes first.

**Files.** Each shard a broker holds keeps six files open. At 1,500 members that is 9,000 files, and a process usually starts with a soft limit of 1,024. The broker raises its soft limit to the hard limit at start and warns when that is too low.

**Fsyncs.** A Raft log fsyncs on commit, so every busy shard flushes on its own. Handing each flush to Tokio's blocking pool put them all in one shared queue, which got slower the more logs flushed at once, so each log has a thread that runs only its flushes and exits after 10 s of quiet. An opt-in io_uring path sends every log's flushes through one ring. It is off by default: in a Linux VM on a 16-vCPU laptop, one log's flush took about 47 µs through the ring against 24 µs on its own thread ([#2330](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2330)). It becomes the default only if a run with many busy logs shows it winning.

**Thread hops.** A write wakes several tasks in turn: the publish task, openraft's core, its state machine worker, its IO forwarder and the replication streams. On a multi-threaded runtime each wake can be a hop to another worker. So a broker runs groups on a few single-threaded runtimes, one per four cores and at most 8, and openraft spawns all of a group's tasks on the thread it was started from, without any change to openraft. A wake between them is a push onto a local queue, and one wake-up of the thread runs every ready task of every group on it. On 16-core hosts with four group threads, CPU per message dropped 12% to 33% across the benchmark cells.

## What we would tell anyone doing this

- **Own the clock.** One timer per process that knows when each group is next due. A group that is hearing its leader should cost nothing between messages.
- **Count wakes.** An idle group's cost is how often it wakes times what each wake does. Both numbers are worth a profile.
- **Batch per peer, and price the straggler.** Batching couples groups. Pick how long one group may hold the rest, from what a real stall looks like.
- **Bound elections on the shared disk.** A vote is an fsync. Thousands of them inside one timeout will feed on themselves unless something limits how many start.
- **Multiply every default.** Queue capacities, caches, file handles and flush threads that are right for one group are wrong for thousands. Give them a shared budget.
- **Fix the library where it is the library.** Most of the upstream changes here were small and general. They help every process that hosts more than one group, not only ours.

How many groups one broker should carry is a measurement, not something this post can settle. The plan is a ladder of group counts that reports a broker's base footprint plus a cost per group for memory, idle CPU, disk and files ([#2243](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/issues/2243)). The rules behind everything above are in [per-shard Raft](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/blob/main/docs/per-shard-raft.md), under "One timer for every group", "Group runtime" and "Heartbeats sent together".
