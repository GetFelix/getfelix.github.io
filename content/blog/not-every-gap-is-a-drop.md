Every event on a durable Felix stream carries an offset. A reader uses it for two things. It keeps the last one so it can resume from the next after a reconnect. And it compares one offset with the one before to see whether it missed anything.

The second use only works if you know what a gap means. In [One slow subscriber shouldn't slow anyone else](/blog/one-slow-subscriber.html) we said a jump in offsets is a drop. That's still the rule, with one correction: some offsets never held an event in the first place. This post is about those offsets, why they exist, and how a reader tells them apart from records it really lost.

## Where the empty offsets come from

Each shard of a durable stream is a Raft group, and a record's offset is its index in the Raft log. One record is one entry at one index.

Raft also writes entries that no client asked for. When a member wins an election, it appends a blank entry in its new term, and Felix doesn't let it serve the shard until that entry commits. That's how a new leader knows it holds everything committed before it. A change to the set of replicas is an entry too. Both sit in the same log as the records, so both take an index, and so both take an offset.

Felix puts a few things of its own in that log. A retention trim goes through it, so every replica cuts at the same place. Consumer group state lives there. A conditional publish that is refused still uses up its offsets as blanks, so every replica agrees on what sits at each one.

None of these are events. No subscriber is sent them, no consumer group hands them out, and a Kafka fetch steps over them. But each one leaves a hole in the offsets a reader sees.

## Why not keep the offsets dense

There's an obvious way out. Keep a second counter that only moves for real records, and hand readers that instead. [etcd](https://github.com/etcd-io/etcd) does something like this: its revision moves with writes to the key space, not with every Raft entry.

We chose not to. A second counter is a mapping from index to offset, and every replica has to agree on it through every crash, torn tail and snapshot install. Get it wrong once and two replicas give the same offset to different records, or a reader resumes from an offset that means something else on the new leader. With the offset equal to the index there's nothing to keep in step. A record's offset is the same on every member because Raft already guarantees its index is.

The storage side is cheap too. Felix's segment store never rewrites a record, and an entry that holds no event is stored like any other and skipped on the way out. A hole costs one small record in a segment, and readers step over it.

What it costs is that offsets aren't dense any more, and a reader can't treat every jump as a drop. So the broker has to tell it which jumps are which.

## Telling the reader what was skipped

Every delivered event carries `skipped_before`: how many offsets directly below it hold no event. It's zero almost all the time. It's non-zero on the first event after a run of entries that aren't events, such as the first record a new leader writes.

For two events in a row at `previous` and `offset`, the number of events dropped is

<pre class="code" tabindex="0"><code>dropped = offset - previous - 1 - skipped_before</code></pre>

If that's zero, the gap was all bookkeeping. If it's more, the reader missed that many records. For the first event of a subscription, count from the `start_offset` the broker reported instead: `offset - start_offset - skipped_before`.

The count is worked out where the empty entries are seen. On the leader, the Raft state machine walks each run of entries it applies, and for each record it notes how many entries just below it held nothing. A publisher reserves its place in that table before it proposes its batch and reads its count once the batch commits, so a count it still needs can't be thrown away by the runs that apply in between. A follower delivers to its own subscribers from its copy of the log, and there the count is simply the distance from the last offset it delivered to the next record it reads.

We hold the broker to this arithmetic in our own tests. The history checker behind our fault-injection runs walks the final read of every list and requires each offset to equal the one before it, plus one, plus its `skipped_before`. Any other jump fails the run.

The field is negotiated, like everything else on the wire. A client opts in with a flag bit, and the broker sets the count only on a batch that follows a skip, so every other frame is byte for byte what an older client gets. A client that didn't opt in sees each leader change as one dropped event. That's a false alarm, but it's a loud one, and loud is the right way to be wrong.

## When the count is off, it's off one way

There are a few places where the number isn't exact. Each of them makes a reader see a drop that didn't happen, and none of them hides one that did.

`skipped_before` only describes the offsets right below its own event. If the event carrying it is the one that was dropped, the next gap includes those skipped offsets and the reader overcounts. It still sees a drop. Only the size is off.

The runs are counted in memory. After a member restarts or installs a snapshot, the first record above that point can be told too few, by the blank entries just below it. The reader sees a small drop.

A leader that loses its term in the middle of a publish can't tell whether that batch committed. It doesn't deliver it, and its live subscribers see those offsets as a drop. The log knows the answer. A reader that goes back to it gets the records if they committed and nothing if they didn't.

What the broker must never do is report more skipped offsets than there were, because that would pass off a real drop as bookkeeping. Every way the count goes wrong above makes it come up short. None makes it come up long.

## What a client should do with a gap

**Count the drop, not the gap.** Subtract `skipped_before` every time. A reader that compares raw offsets will page someone after every failover.

**Treat a drop as a reason to look.** The records are in the log. Subscribe again from `previous + 1` and read forward, keeping only offsets you haven't seen. If the drop was one of the false ones above, nothing new comes back before `offset`, and all it cost was one extra read.

**Let the broker say it first.** A client that offers the lagged feature has its subscription ended at the first record its queue dropped, with `subscription_lagged` naming the offset to resume from. That covers the one case offsets can't: a drop with nothing published after it, where there's no next event to show the gap.

**Don't do arithmetic across offsets.** `last - first + 1` isn't a record count on a Raft shard. If you need one, count the events.

The Rust, Python, TypeScript, C and .NET clients all expose `skipped_before` on their event types.

## Every log that does this meets the same choice

This isn't special to Felix. Any system whose offsets come from a consensus log ends up with entries no client wrote, and has to pick. It can keep a second, dense counter and own the mapping through every crash. Or it can show the index and tell readers what's in the holes. Kafka readers already step over offsets taken by transaction markers and by compaction, so gaps alone aren't new to anyone who has read a partition.

We took the second option. The mapping is one more thing that has to be right after every failure, and a skip count is a small, checkable fact that rides along with the event it describes. Offsets stay the same on every replica, and a gap still tells you something: either the broker says why it's there, or it's a drop.

The wire format for the count is in the [protocol docs](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/blob/main/docs/protocol.md#offsets-that-hold-no-event), and the overflow policies that cause real drops are in the [broker configuration docs](https://github.com/GetFelix/felix/blob/main/docs/broker-config.md).
