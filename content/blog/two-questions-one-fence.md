A shard of three replicas loses its leader, and two of the three brokers are healthy. Both are up, both are reachable, and both are telling the truth. The shard still cannot elect a leader, because the new leader's fence will not count either of them.

A fence that asks one combined question can stall this way in a handful of different situations. Each stall has its own cause and its own fix, and each fix is a special case in a test that is already hard to read. This post walks through what that test is really checking, why it is two checks wearing one coat, and the rule Felix uses instead.

## A few terms first

A **shard** is one slice of a stream, kept by a **replica set** of brokers. One of them is the **leader**. Each time leadership changes hands, the shard's **generation** goes up. Before a new leader opens at generation G, it **fences**: it asks members of the replica set to promise that they will refuse anything from an older generation, and to say what their logs hold.

A member's promise is a **ballot**, a record that names the generation it promised and the sender it promised it to. Ballots are fsynced, so a broker that crashes and restarts still remembers every promise it made. A broker that loses its data directory does not. A wiped disk or a restored backup forgets ballots along with everything else, and the broker comes back willing to make promises that contradict the ones it made before.

## The test did two jobs

A new leader opening at G has to establish two separate things.

The first is **exclusion**. Nothing older may still be able to acknowledge a write, and no second leader may open at G. A majority of the replica set promising G, with ballots that cannot be forgotten, does it. If an old leader later tries to get a majority of the same set to acknowledge a write, the two majorities share a member, and that member refuses it. Only ballots matter here. What the member's log contains is irrelevant to whether its promise holds.

The second is **coverage**. The new leader must hold every write that was acknowledged before it. Here only logs matter. If a majority acknowledged a write and the new leader hears from a majority, some member it heard from holds the write, and the leader takes the log that is furthest ahead. What the member promised is irrelevant to whether its log is complete.

The older Felix fence counted an answer only when one predicate held: the answer came from the right copy of the data, the copy was not under doubt after an unclean crash, and on a continuing fence a ballot named the sender. That is a ballot condition, a log condition and an identity condition in one expression. It erred in the safe direction every time, but it said no in cases where each question on its own would have said yes.

Each of those cases was fixed by adding an exception. A short copy was refused. A copy acknowledged by two was allowed back. A learner's answer was refused. A narrow window let a continuing leader count a ballot. Identity pins recorded which copy each member was supposed to be. Every one was a reasonable reaction to a real defect, and every one made the next stall harder to see.

## One stall, in detail

Three brokers, a, b and c. Broker a leads generation 6, then gets partitioned away. Broker b stands for generation 7 and wins on b and c. a never hears the election.

Then b is killed. The control plane adopts generation 7 and fails over to c at generation 8. Now c has to fence.

Under the older rule the control plane pins each member to the copy of the data it last reported, a storage id. Its last report from b names only c. It has nothing for a, because a never heard the election and b, newly elected, carried no id for it. So the fence pins a to the empty id.

a is back and answers c's fence honestly. Its ballot is intact, its directory is the one it always had, and it says so by reporting its id, 37df. The fence compares 37df against the empty id on record, finds no match, and discards the answer. b is dead. c counts one answer out of three, and the shard sits there with a healthy majority.

Nothing is wrong with a. It is a displaced leader that never crashed, and its promise is as good as any. The test asked a question about identity, and the answer was not what the fence needed.

## The two halves, one at a time

The new rule asks the two questions separately, and each has its own evidence.

**Exclusion counts ballots, and only ballots.** A member's promise counts if its ballots are *kept*, meaning its directory never lost them: no wipe, no restore, no replaced copy. An unclean crash does not matter, because ballots are fsynced. It does not matter which log the copy holds or what its storage id is. A broker started without the control plane attests neither kept ballots nor a trusted copy until its restore check has run. A restored directory looks clean until the broker can compare it with the control plane's record, so counting it early is unsafe, and the model checks below produce the counterexamples. Once the check passes, the broker attests like any other start. The price is some availability while the control plane is down, which starting without it already accepts.

A member that did lose its ballots counts again once a newer replica set names the new copy, its leader has shipped to it, and the set was written after the loss. The control plane also keeps a durable record that a leader brought the replaced copy level, and the copy checks that record against its own log when it answers. The record can stand in for the leader's vouch, but it never makes a promise count. That credit is bounded. An elected leader can open before a copy is lost and have its generation adopted later for a set that names the replacement, so the generation start would come before records the old copy acknowledged. The credit therefore applies only while replica election is not finalized across the fleet. Once it is, a copy that lost its ballots counts for no set.

This is safe because the old leader needs a majority of its own set to acknowledge anything. The new fence collects a kept ballot from a majority of every set that could still acknowledge, which is the fence's own set plus each set started since the last generation with evidence that it opened. Two majorities of one set share a kept ballot, and that ballot is already at G or above, so it refuses the old leader. A set is never dropped on silence.

**Coverage counts logs, and only logs.** A copy is *trusted* when it holds everything its seat acknowledged. That is true when the process started clean, or when it crashed without losing its page cache. It is also true when a leader has since shipped it a batch that left it level with the later of two points: the start record of that leader's own generation, and the leader's commit mark, which has to be above zero. Both matter. Right after an open the commit mark can sit below records an earlier generation acknowledged, so the mark alone would vouch a copy that is missing them. The start record alone falls short of what the leader acknowledged since.

An idle shard needs care too. The batch that brings a follower level goes out before the follower's own answer raises the mark, so it carries a mark of zero and vouches for nothing, and a level follower is sent nothing more. Left alone, that follower would stay untrusted until the next client write. So the leader sends a level follower its last record again, with the current mark, until one such batch has landed. The follower is trusted again with no writes at all. For a copy that lost its ballots, the leader doing the vouching must also be the one that readmitted it. The fence needs trusted answers that meet every majority of every set it fences. If a majority acknowledged a write, a trusted answer from the majority the leader hears from holds it, and the leader takes the log that is furthest ahead.

Two cases need care. A leader that is *continuing*, still the same process that opened G-1, with a ballot that still names it and no failed flush since, already holds everything it acknowledged and skips coverage. And a copy that is not trusted may still answer, and its log may still be the furthest ahead. The leader takes the log furthest ahead by last generation and length. An untrusted answer just does not count toward the meet.

Trust has to survive a restart. A copy that crashed uncleanly, answered a fence and then restarted cleanly would look like any clean copy, because the ballot it took replaced whatever said it was short. So each broker keeps a small durable `copy-trust` record at the root of its data directory. A start that rotates the storage id first writes it saying no copy is trusted, and each vouch adds a copy, synced, before the copy attests trusted. The same record names the copies the broker led before a crash, written before such a copy takes a newer leader's ballot, so a crashed leader's copy stays doubted across a clean restart. It also names a learner's copy that took a ballot while its promises counted for nothing, so that ballot stays unkept until a newer set readmits it. A record that is lost or does not decode marks every copy all three ways.

The two halves do not share evidence. A broker that crashed uncleanly is untrusted for coverage, because its tail may be gone. It is still kept for exclusion, because its ballots are not. In the old test it was one doubted copy. In the new rule it is a full vote on one question and no vote on the other.

## The open rule

A leader opens at G when, for each set in the fence, a majority of that set answered with a kept ballot, and unless it is continuing, the trusted answers meet every majority of that set. That is the condition for a failover, an election, a rename, a skipped generation, a set change, a readmission and a continuing leader. Each of those is the same rule applied to a different list of sets. When a change of set has a staged learner, the old set is counted twice, with and without the learner, because a leader that counted the learner may have acknowledged on a majority of the larger set. The larger set needs only half of its members to have promised, which meets every majority of it, so one voter that cannot answer does not stall the fence for good. The set list is the fence's own set plus every set started since the last generation known to have opened, and a set is never dropped on silence.

Back to the stall. a is kept, so it counts for exclusion no matter what id it reports. It started clean, so it is trusted. c is trusted too. With three members, two trusted answers meet every majority (`2 * trusted >= voters`), and c opens. The empty pin is never consulted.

Brokers decide this with each replica's own attestation: whether it kept its ballots and whether it is trusted. The rule is on by default and `FELIX_UNIFIED_FENCE=0` turns it off. It needs a durable directory, so a memory-only broker counts as it always did. A peer that predates the attestation sends an answer with none in it, and that answer is judged by the older rule, both as a promise and as coverage. That is why a rolling upgrade is safe in either order. Group and counter logs carry no attestation and keep counting by the pins.

## The one time it still waits

There is a case where waiting is correct, and the fence will not talk itself out of it. A shard of three: b leads generation 2 and acknowledges a write on b and c, which is a majority. Then b crashes uncleanly and loses the end of its log, and c goes down. Only a is left, and a never saw the write. The fence at generation 3 gets a kept ballot from a and b, so exclusion is satisfied. But only a is trusted, and a alone does not cover every majority.

Why not open on a? Because from the answers, a seat that was never given the write looks the same as one that acknowledged it and then went down. The write could be on b and c, b has lost its copy, and c is silent. If a opened, that acknowledged write would be gone, and the client was told it was safe. Waiting costs availability. Opening would cost data.

Bringing c back ends the wait: once c answers with a trusted log, the write is found. Giving up on c for good and accepting that an acknowledged write may be lost is a decision for a person who knows what happened to the node. The fence cannot make that call from the inside. It takes two faults to get here, an unclean crash and a second copy down, and the same wait happens when the lone trusted copy is perfectly intact, because the fence cannot tell.

## How it was checked

The rule has its own TLA+ model in `docs/formal/FelixShardUnified.tla`. It models seats and the copies that change under them: unclean crashes, wipes and restores, plus elections, failovers, renames, skipped generations and set changes. TLC explores every interleaving within the limits a config sets, and the invariants it checks include that no generation has two leaders and that a leader holds every record acknowledged before it.

A model that only passes proves little, so most of the configs are built to fail. Each one switches off one part of the rule and TLC must find the defect that part prevented. Counting a wiped or restored copy's old ballots produces a second leader at one generation. Counting a copy that was only registered, not caught up, produces a leader missing an acknowledged record. So do counting a doubted copy for coverage, skipping coverage, dropping a set that never opened without evidence from its own leader, vouching a copy without the commit mark, and vouching at the mark without the start record. Two more configs run the old predicate in outline and fail the liveness property that a shard with a healthy majority eventually serves.

The passing configs cover failovers, elections, set changes, restores, readmissions, idle shards and joint fences with learners. Two of them, readmission and the evidenced drop of an unopened set, run without client writes to stay tractable, so they check coverage only against start records. The one-write versions run in a nightly job.

The checks shaped the rule. A vouch at the start record alone is short of what the leader acknowledged since, and a vouch at the commit mark alone can sit below records an earlier generation acknowledged right after an open, so the vouch has to reach the later of the two. A copy that lost its ballots can only be vouched for by a leader that readmitted it. A restored directory also looks clean to a broker that started without the control plane, which is why such a broker attests nothing until its restore check has run.

## What it costs

A copy that lost its log needs a leader to bring it level before it counts for coverage, and one that lost its ballots needs a set that readmits it before it counts for exclusion. The rule puts both in the answer a copy gives, so the leader no longer infers them from which copy a record says it should be.

Three costs are real. A broker started without the control plane counts for nothing until its restore check has run, which needs the control plane. A shard with one trusted copy and every other member doubted or down waits, as described above. And an acknowledgement from a copy that lost its ballots does not count until it has been readmitted.

A client sees the same thing as before: a write is acknowledged when a majority holds it. The fence decides who may lead.
